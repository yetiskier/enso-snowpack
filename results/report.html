El Niño and the Ski Season

What the Oceanic Niño Index actually predicts about powder days, storm frequency and base depth across 42 ski regions of the western United States — and, just as usefully, where it predicts nothing at all.

The short version

Four findings

1. The effect is real but geographically narrow. Of 971 tests across every region, window of winter and measure, 51 survive false-discovery control. Nearly all of them sit in the northern Rockies and the Cascades, where El Niño costs roughly a foot of snow, or in New Mexico and Arizona, where it adds snow in spring.

2. For most of the West, El Niño predicts nothing. The Wasatch, the I-70 corridor, Tahoe, the San Juans, Sun Valley and the Elk Mountains never clear significance on any measure in any window. That is a finding, not a gap.

3. The Christmas–New Year window behaves differently from the rest of winter. Every other window follows a north–south seesaw. The holidays lean toward less snow in 103 of 123 tests, in all three latitude bands at once, including the southern ranges that otherwise gain.

4. The strength of an event carries no information. Tested directly across 2619 region-window-measure combinations, nothing survives. A very strong El Niño delivers more powder days than a strong one.

Reading the largest effects

Montana Snowbowl in midwinter is the single most affected place in the study: twelve inches less new snow, two fewer storm days, and 45 % fewer powder days. The largest gain anywhere is northern New Mexico in spring, which picks up nearly nine additional days with a skiable base.

Geography

Where the effect lives

The turnover sits near 40–42° N, running through central Colorado and Utah. North of it El Niño suppresses snowfall; south of it, in spring, it adds. The southern half of the seesaw is weaker and less reliable than the northern half — several southern regions show a positive tendency that never reaches significance.

Geography with uncertainty

The effect moves through the winter

A single number per region hides how firmly it is pinned, so every ski region here carries its whole probability distribution rather than a colour. The dashed hairline through each glyph is no change. A density astride that line is a region where El Niño does nothing measurable.

Drawn window by window, the maps show what a season-long average cannot: the effect does not sit still. It is nearly absent in the early season, seizes the interior ranges over the holidays, moves to the northern Rockies and Cascades in midwinter, and retreats to the Cascades alone by spring. A region can be firmly hit in one window and indistinguishable from zero in the next.

Early season · 1 November – 15 December

Almost nothing is decided. Whatever El Niño does to a season has barely begun by mid-December.

Holidays · 16 December – 5 January

The widest, most coherent signal of the winter, and it lands on the interior: the Wasatch, the Uintas, the Elk Mountains, the Park and Snowy ranges, the Rubies. These are the same regions that show nothing in midwinter.

Midwinter · 6 January – 28 February

The signal migrates north, to the Bitterroots, the Missoula ranges, the Coeur d'Alenes, Whitefish and the Washington Cascades, with northern New Mexico gaining. The interior ranges that were firmly hit over the holidays are now undecided.

Spring · 1 March – 15 April

Only the Cascades and central Oregon remain decided.

Two standards of evidence, and why they disagree

These maps judge each region on its own: does its 95 % interval exclude zero? The headline tables apply Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all 971 tests at once, which is far stricter, because asking a question of 41 regions in four windows on six measures will turn up apparent effects by chance alone.

That is why a region can appear here at −50 % over the holidays and not appear in the list of results that survive correction. The per-region interval answers "what does the record say about this range"; the corrected grid answers "which of these findings would I bet on". A region that clears the first but not the second is worth watching, not worth relying on.

The negative result

Event strength carries no information

Phase says which way a winter leans. Strength says how far the index went. The two are routinely conflated in seasonal forecasting, so strength was tested head-on rather than inferred: every region, window and measure was asked whether a larger event means less snow among El Niño winters only, the same among La Niña winters, and whether adding the index value to a phase-only model explains more.

Practical consequence

Plan for the phase and ignore the magnitude. The three very strong events on record (1983, 1998, 2016) were not the worst winters in the northern Rockies, and the strong events (1958, 1966, 1973, 1992, 2010, 2024) were not reliably worse than the weak ones.

Method

How this was measured

A powder day is six inches of snow

A fixed water threshold is a maritime yardstick. Cascade new snow runs about 12 % density and Colorado's about 7 %, so 25 mm of water is roughly eight inches of Cascade snow but fourteen inches of Colorado snow — counting water alone makes the driest, lightest snowpacks look storm-free. Each station's own new-snow ratio is measured from the overlap of its depth and water records, then applied to its much longer water record. Across 508 stations the median ratio is 7.5 inches of snow per inch of water.

Regions are ranges, not states

A state boundary is not a snow boundary. Tahoe spans California and Nevada, the Bitterroots and Selkirks span Montana and Idaho, the Tetons span Wyoming and Idaho, the Sangre de Cristo spans Colorado and New Mexico. Each region is anchored on a mountain range around real ski terrain and labelled with its snow climate.

Stations are weighted by elevation

A gauge represents a ski area only insofar as it shares its snow climate, and elevation is most of that. Each region carries its served base-to-summit band; stations are weighted by a Gaussian on the vertical gap to that band and another on horizontal distance, so a valley gauge below the lifts does not speak for a mountain.

Significance, and the correction for asking often

The modified bootstrap of Brown & Harper (2026): omit a random 20 % of winters, refit, repeat ten thousand times, and require the 2σ bounds of the resulting near-Gaussian distribution to exclude zero. That raw rule fires on about 30 % of pure noise, because the subsample spread understates the sampling error by the delete-d factor √((n−d)/d) = 2, so a calibrated version and a permutation p-value are reported alongside. Benjamini–Hochberg control is then applied across the entire grid.

Two views of the same data

The statistical tests run on per-station detrended z-scores, so a long-term warming trend cannot pose as an ENSO signal. The physical composites are raw averages by phase — what actually happened. The two are matched explicitly on region, window and measure rather than assumed to line up.

Windows that carry a season

Early season (1 Nov – 15 Dec), Holidays (16 Dec – 5 Jan), Midwinter (6 Jan – 28 Feb) and Spring (1 Mar – 15 Apr). They abut without gaps. These replace the hydrologic periods used in water-supply work, which are built around runoff timing rather than revenue.

Coverage

Regions and station coverage

How a region's location is fixed

Each region's centre is computed from the coordinates of the ski areas it serves, never typed by hand. An earlier draft did type them, and drifted: one region's centre sat 61 km from Schweitzer, and another grouped Lookout Pass with Montana Snowbowl 138 km away under a name matching neither. The regions are now split where their areas were too far apart to share a snow climate — the Selkirks from the Coeur d'Alenes, Bridger Bowl from Big Sky, Wolf Creek from Telluride, Taos from Santa Fe — and a test checks that every named ski area falls inside a state its region claims.

Effective station count is Kish's measure applied to the elevation weights. Regions with few stations inside the served band — notably southern Utah and the Snowy Range — should be read with more caution, because SNOTEL does not reach those elevations locally.

Limits

What this does not establish

  • An r² of 15–35 % is real and it is not a forecast. ENSO shifts the odds for a season. Most of the year-to-year variance remains weather.
  • Snow depth records begin around 1993. New-snow ratios are calibrated on that period and applied backward, which assumes density has been stable. Depth-based composites rest on roughly 25 winters against 45 to 60 for the water-based ones.
  • A SNOTEL gauge is not a ski area. Even with elevation weighting these are nearby mountain stations, and they say nothing about aspect, grooming or snowmaking.
  • Overlapping regions are not fully independent where their radii intersect, so the sign tests read slightly optimistically.
  • Six inches is a choice. Regions with few powder days in absolute terms show large percentage swings on small differences.
  • The strength null is bounded by power. Within-phase tests had about sixteen winters and could only have detected a correlation above roughly 0.65. The claim is that across 2619 chances nothing rose above noise, and the better-powered nested test also found nothing.
